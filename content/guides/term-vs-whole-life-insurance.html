Life insurance comes in two broad families: term, which covers you for a set number of years, and permanent, which is designed to last your whole life and often builds cash value. Both can be the right choice, but for different people and different goals. This guide explains how each works in plain language so you can have a clearer conversation with a licensed advisor.

How term life insurance works

Term life insurance is the simplest form of coverage. You pay a premium, and if you die during the policy's term, your beneficiaries receive the death benefit. If you outlive the term, the coverage ends and nothing is paid out.

  • Common terms: 10, 15, 20, 25, or 30 years. In Canada, 10- and 20-year terms are especially common, along with "term to 100" products that behave more like permanent coverage.
  • Level premiums: most term policies keep the same premium for the whole term, then rise sharply if renewed.
  • Renewable and convertible options: many policies let you renew without a new medical exam, or convert to permanent coverage within a set window.
  • No cash value: term is pure protection. You are paying for the death benefit only.

Term is designed for temporary needs: the years when you have a mortgage, young children, or a household that depends on your income.

How permanent life insurance works

Permanent life insurance is intended to stay in force for your entire life, as long as premiums are paid as required. Most permanent policies combine a death benefit with a savings-like component called cash value. The two most common types are whole life and universal life.

Whole life

Whole life usually offers fixed premiums, a guaranteed death benefit, and cash value that grows at a guaranteed rate set out in the contract. Some whole life policies are "participating," meaning they may pay dividends based on the insurer's results. Dividends are not guaranteed, but when paid they can be taken as cash, used to reduce premiums, or used to buy additional coverage.

Universal life

Universal life offers more flexibility. Within limits, you can adjust premiums and sometimes the death benefit. Cash value growth depends on the policy design: some credit a declared interest rate, others are linked to a market index, and variable versions let you choose investment options with more risk and potential reward. That flexibility comes with responsibility. If premiums are too low or returns disappoint, a universal life policy can lapse unless you pay more later.

Cost differences and how cash value works

For the same death benefit, permanent coverage typically costs many times more than term, especially when you are young and healthy. The reason is straightforward. A term insurer only pays if you die within the term, and most people do not. A permanent insurer expects to pay eventually, and part of each premium also funds the cash value.

Cash value is often misunderstood. Here is what it generally means:

  • It grows slowly at first. In the early years, much of the premium goes to insurance costs, commissions, and fees, so cash value can be small.
  • Growth is often tax-advantaged. In both the US and Canada, cash value generally grows on a tax-deferred or tax-sheltered basis within certain limits. Specific rules differ by country, and withdrawals or surrenders can trigger tax.
  • You can access it. Policy loans and withdrawals are possible, but unpaid loans and withdrawals usually reduce the death benefit, and loans accrue interest.
  • Surrendering early can be costly. Many policies have surrender charges in the first years, so cancelling early may return far less than you paid in.
Key takeaway: Term buys the most protection per dollar for a defined period. Permanent buys lifelong protection and a savings component, at a much higher cost. Match the policy to how long the need actually lasts.

Who each type tends to fit

Term often fits people who

  • Have dependents and a need that will shrink over time, such as raising children or paying off a mortgage.
  • Want the largest death benefit their budget allows.
  • Prefer to keep insurance and investing separate.
  • Expect to be largely self-insured by retirement through savings and paid-off debts.

Permanent may fit people who

  • Have a lifelong need, such as supporting a dependent with a disability or funding a special needs trust.
  • Want to leave an inheritance regardless of when they die, or cover estate taxes or final taxes on assets.
  • Own a business and need coverage for succession or buy-sell planning.
  • Have maximized other tax-advantaged savings and want an additional option, with the budget to keep premiums going long term.

Some households use a mix: a larger term policy for the high-need years plus a smaller permanent policy for final expenses or legacy goals.

The "buy term and invest the difference" debate

A popular strategy suggests buying affordable term coverage and investing the money you would have spent on a permanent policy. Supporters and critics both make reasonable points.

Arguments in favour

  • Term premiums are much lower, freeing up money for retirement accounts and other investments.
  • Investments held separately can be more transparent, flexible, and lower cost than policy cash value.
  • Most people's need for life insurance declines as they build savings, so lifelong coverage may be unnecessary.

Arguments against

  • The strategy only works if the difference is actually invested, consistently, for decades. Many people do not follow through.
  • Investment returns are not guaranteed, while some permanent policies offer guarantees.
  • If a lifelong need appears later, buying new coverage at an older age or with health issues can be expensive or unavailable.
  • For high earners who have used up other tax-advantaged room, permanent insurance may offer planning benefits that term cannot.

Neither side is right for everyone. The honest answer depends on your discipline, your time horizon, your tax situation, and whether your need is temporary or permanent.

Riders and final expense insurance

Riders are optional add-ons that customize a policy, usually for an extra cost. Availability and terms vary by insurer and jurisdiction. Common examples include:

  • Waiver of premium: premiums are waived if you become disabled and cannot work, as defined in the policy.
  • Accelerated death benefit: allows early access to part of the benefit if you are diagnosed with a terminal illness, and sometimes certain chronic or critical illnesses.
  • Child term rider: adds a small amount of coverage for children.
  • Guaranteed insurability: lets you buy more coverage at set future dates without new medical underwriting.
  • Accidental death benefit: pays an extra amount if death results from a covered accident.

Final expense (burial) insurance

Final expense insurance is a small permanent policy, often in the range of a few thousand to a few tens of thousands of dollars, meant to cover funeral costs and final bills. It is commonly marketed to older adults and may require little or no medical underwriting. Simplified or guaranteed-issue versions are easier to qualify for but typically cost more per dollar of coverage, and many include a waiting period during which only premiums plus interest are refunded if death occurs from natural causes. Compare carefully against setting money aside in savings or a prepaid funeral arrangement.

Questions to ask an advisor

  1. How long do you expect my need for coverage to last, and why?
  2. What would equivalent term coverage cost compared with the permanent option you are suggesting?
  3. Which values in this illustration are guaranteed, and which are projections?
  4. What fees, surrender charges, and cost-of-insurance increases apply?
  5. What happens if I miss a premium or want to reduce payments later?
  6. Can this term policy be converted to permanent coverage, and until what age?
  7. How are you compensated for recommending this policy?

Frequently asked questions

Is term life insurance a waste of money if I outlive it?

Not necessarily. Like home or car insurance, you are paying for protection during the years your family needed it. Outliving the term usually means your loved ones were never left without support.

Can I switch from term to permanent later?

Many term policies include a conversion option that lets you switch to permanent coverage without a new medical exam, typically before a certain age or within a set period. Check your contract for the exact terms.

Is cash value the same as an investment account?

No. Cash value is part of an insurance contract, with its own fees, rules, and tax treatment. It can be useful, but it is generally less flexible than a regular investment account and should be evaluated on its own terms.

Does my beneficiary get the cash value and the death benefit?

With most whole life policies, beneficiaries receive the death benefit, and the cash value does not pay out separately. Some universal life designs allow an increasing death benefit that includes cash value. Ask how your specific policy works.

Is final expense insurance worth it?

It can help people who cannot qualify for other coverage or who want certainty that funeral costs are covered. Because it can be costly per dollar of benefit and may have waiting periods, compare it with saving directly or a prepaid funeral plan.
