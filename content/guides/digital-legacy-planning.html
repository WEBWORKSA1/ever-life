Much of modern life now lives online: bank statements, family photos, messages, subscriptions and sometimes significant investments. When someone dies or becomes incapacitated, those assets can be locked behind passwords, two-factor codes and terms of service that even a legally appointed executor struggles to get past. A little planning today turns a frustrating digital maze into a clear path for the people you trust.

What counts as your digital legacy

Your digital legacy includes every account, file and online asset you own or control. Some have financial value, some have sentimental value, and some simply need to be closed so they stop billing a card or exposing personal information. A useful way to think about them is in four groups:

  • Financial: online banking, brokerage and retirement portals, payment apps, cryptocurrency, loyalty points and online businesses.
  • Communication: email, phone accounts, messaging apps and social media profiles.
  • Memories and creative work: photos, videos, cloud storage, blogs, writing and creative portfolios.
  • Utilities and subscriptions: streaming, software, domain names, utilities with paperless billing and anything with auto-renewal.

Email deserves special attention. It is often the "master key" used to reset passwords for nearly everything else, so access to your primary email account can unlock, or block, the rest of your digital estate.

Step one: build an account inventory

Start with a simple list. You do not need to record passwords in the inventory itself; in fact, it is safer not to. Instead, note what exists and what you want done with it.

  • Name of the service and the type of account
  • The email or username used to sign in (not the password)
  • Whether it holds money, memories, or neither
  • Your wishes: transfer, download and keep, memorialize, or close
  • Any auto-payments linked to it

Store the inventory with your other estate documents and mention it in your letter of instruction. Do not put passwords or account numbers in your will, which may become a public record after probate. Our free legacy planning checklist includes a section for listing digital accounts.

Password managers and emergency access

A password manager is one of the most practical tools for digital legacy planning. It stores your credentials in an encrypted vault protected by one master password. Many password managers now offer an emergency access or trusted contact feature. You nominate a person who can request access to your vault; if you do not decline the request within a waiting period you choose, they are granted access.

If your password manager lacks this feature, or you prefer not to use one, alternatives include:

  • Writing your master password and device passcodes on paper, sealed and stored with your will or in a safe place your executor knows about.
  • Keeping backup codes for two-factor authentication in the same secure location, since a trusted person may not have your phone.
  • Updating these records whenever you change critical passwords.

Be aware that logging into someone else's account with their password, even with permission, may technically violate a platform's terms of service or local computer access laws. Legal authority, explained below, is the safer route for anything significant.

Key takeaway: Protect your primary email and your phone passcode above all else. Whoever can reach those can usually recover almost every other account, so plan carefully for who gets that access and how.

Built-in legacy contact and inactive account features

Major email, phone and social platforms offer tools that let you decide in advance what happens to your account. They generally fall into three types:

  • Legacy contact: you name a person who can access certain data, such as photos and files, after your death, usually after providing a death certificate and a special access key.
  • Inactive account manager: you set a period of inactivity, after which the service notifies chosen contacts, shares selected data with them, or deletes the account.
  • Memorialization: social profiles can be converted into a remembrance page, sometimes managed by a designated person, or deleted at the family's request.

These settings are usually buried in privacy or account sections. Set them up for your main email, phone ecosystem and any social networks you use. In the US legal framework described below, these online tools generally take priority over instructions in your will, so they matter more than many people realize.

Cryptocurrency and online financial accounts

Traditional online accounts

Online banks and brokerages are regulated financial institutions. Your executor can typically access these accounts with a death certificate and court documents, just as with any bank. The challenge is knowing the accounts exist. List them in your inventory and, where available, add payable-on-death or transfer-on-death beneficiaries.

Cryptocurrency

Crypto is different. If assets sit in a self-custody wallet, whoever holds the private keys or recovery phrase controls the funds. If that phrase is lost, the assets are effectively gone forever; no court can order them recovered. If they sit on an exchange, the exchange may have its own estate process, which can be slow.

  • Record which wallets and exchanges you use and roughly what they hold.
  • Store recovery phrases securely and offline, never in plain email or unencrypted notes.
  • Consider splitting secrets or using a lawyer or safe deposit box so no single person can misuse them while you are alive.
  • Leave simple instructions; your heirs may not be technical.
  • Ask an advisor about tax reporting for inherited digital assets in your country.

Photos, memories and memorialization choices

For many families, photos and videos are the most treasured digital assets. Cloud storage is convenient, but accounts can be closed after long inactivity or when subscriptions lapse. Consider these steps:

  • Periodically download important photos and keep at least one offline backup.
  • Use shared family albums so key memories are not held in a single account.
  • Label or caption meaningful images so future generations know who and where they show.
  • Decide whether you want social profiles memorialized, deleted, or left as they are, and write that down.

Some people also record voice messages, video letters or written reflections for loved ones. These can be stored with your letter of instruction and released by your executor.

Legal considerations: RUFADAA and beyond

In the United States, most states have adopted the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (RUFADAA), a model law developed by the Uniform Law Commission. In broad terms, it creates a priority order for whose instructions count:

  1. Your choices in a platform's online tool, such as a legacy contact setting, come first.
  2. If you did not use an online tool, directions in your will, trust or power of attorney apply.
  3. If you left no directions, the platform's terms of service govern.

RUFADAA also distinguishes between a "catalogue" of communications (who you emailed and when) and the actual content of messages. Fiduciaries can more readily obtain the catalogue, but access to content generally requires your explicit consent. Including clear digital asset language in your will and power of attorney helps.

In Canada, the Uniform Law Conference of Canada has prepared a model act on fiduciary access to digital assets, but provincial adoption has been limited, so the situation varies. Other countries handle the issue through privacy, contract and succession law in different ways. Ask your estate lawyer how digital assets are treated where you live.

Frequently asked questions

Can I leave my social media accounts to someone in my will?

Usually you cannot transfer ownership, since most platforms license accounts to individuals. You can, however, express wishes about memorialization or deletion and grant your executor authority to act, ideally alongside the platform's own legacy settings.

Should I put my passwords in my will?

No. Wills can become public during probate and are hard to update. Keep passwords in a password manager or a secure sealed document, and reference its location in your letter of instruction.

What happens to purchased music, e-books and movies?

Many digital purchases are licenses tied to your account rather than owned files, and terms often prohibit transfer. Rules vary by service, so check the terms and do not count on passing these on.

How can my family find accounts I forgot to list?

Executors often review email, bank and card statements for recurring charges and account notices. Keeping an up-to-date inventory saves them that detective work and reduces the risk of missed assets.

Do I need a lawyer for digital legacy planning?

Much of the practical work, such as inventories and platform settings, you can do yourself. A licensed lawyer can add proper digital asset language to your will and power of attorney and explain the laws in your state, province or country.
