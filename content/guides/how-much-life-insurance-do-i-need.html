Life insurance has one job: to replace the financial support you provide if you die while others still depend on it. Buy too little and your family may face hard choices at the worst possible time; buy far too much and you pay premiums that could have gone toward savings or living well today. This guide walks through the main ways to estimate a sensible coverage amount, with a worked example you can adapt to your own situation.

Why the coverage amount matters

Most people spend more time choosing a policy type than choosing a policy amount, yet the amount is usually the bigger decision. The death benefit is what your beneficiaries actually receive. It needs to cover immediate costs, pay down obligations you leave behind, and replace income for as long as your household would have relied on it.

A good estimate starts with a simple question: if you died tomorrow, what money would the people who depend on you need, and for how long? Everything below is a structured way of answering that question.

  • Immediate needs: funeral costs, final medical bills, legal and estate settlement expenses.
  • Debts: mortgage, car loans, credit cards, lines of credit, and personal loans.
  • Ongoing needs: the share of household spending your income currently covers.
  • Future goals: children's education, a surviving partner's retirement, care for a dependent adult.

Income-replacement multiples and the "10x income" rule

The quickest shortcut is to multiply your annual income by a fixed number. You will often hear that you should carry about 10 times your salary, and some sources suggest anywhere from 5 to 15 times. These multiples are popular because they are easy to remember and give a ballpark in seconds.

The problem is that a multiple ignores almost everything specific about your life. Two people earning the same salary can have very different needs:

  • A 30-year-old with a new mortgage, two toddlers, and a partner at home may need well over 10 times income.
  • A 58-year-old with a paid-off home, grown children, and solid retirement savings may need far less, or possibly none.
  • A multiple does not account for existing coverage through work, savings you have already built, or government survivor benefits.
  • It also ignores people who earn little or no income but provide valuable unpaid work, such as a stay-at-home parent whose childcare and household management would cost real money to replace.

Treat an income multiple as a sanity check, not an answer. If a more detailed method gives you a figure wildly different from 10 times income, that is a signal to double-check your inputs, not necessarily a sign that either number is wrong.

The DIME method, with a worked example

DIME is a simple framework that breaks your need into four buckets: Debt, Income, Mortgage, and Education. It is more tailored than a flat multiple while still being quick to calculate.

  1. Debt: add up all non-mortgage debts plus an estimate for final expenses such as a funeral.
  2. Income: multiply your annual income by the number of years your family would need support, often until your youngest child is independent.
  3. Mortgage: the remaining balance on your home loan, or an amount to cover rent for a set period if you do not own.
  4. Education: an estimate of what you would like to set aside for each child's post-secondary education.

DIME has limits. It tends to overstate income needs because it does not reduce income for the deceased person's own living costs, taxes, or investment growth on the payout. It also tends to ignore inflation. That is why many planners use it as a starting point and refine from there.

The needs-based method

A needs-based (or "capital needs") analysis is the most detailed approach and the one licensed advisors commonly use. Instead of multiplying income, it builds a picture of what your household would actually spend without you, year by year, and what resources would be available.

Step 1: List lump-sum needs

These are one-time costs at death: final expenses, debts you want paid off, an emergency fund, education funding, and any estate settlement costs.

Step 2: Estimate ongoing annual needs

Start with current household spending, then remove costs that would disappear (your own food, transportation, personal spending) and add costs that might appear (childcare, home maintenance you used to do yourself). Subtract any income the surviving partner will continue to earn.

Step 3: Convert the annual gap into a lump sum

Multiply the annual shortfall by the number of years it will last. More precise versions discount this figure, assuming the lump sum is invested conservatively and adjusted for inflation. An advisor or a good calculator can handle this math for you.

Step 4: Subtract existing resources

Remove existing life insurance, savings, investments your family could reasonably use, and survivor benefits. In the US, Social Security may pay survivor benefits to eligible spouses and children. In Canada, the Canada Pension Plan (and the Quebec Pension Plan in Quebec) offers a death benefit and survivor's pension to eligible contributors' families. Rules and amounts vary, so check the official program details for your situation.

Key takeaway: Start with what your family would need, subtract what they would already have, and the remainder is your coverage gap. Income multiples are a quick check, but a needs-based estimate reflects your actual life.

Subtracting existing assets and coverage

This step is where many estimates go wrong in both directions. Be realistic about which resources truly count.

  • Group life insurance through work is valuable, but it usually ends if you leave your job or retire. Many people choose not to count it fully for long-term planning.
  • Retirement accounts such as a 401(k), IRA, RRSP, or TFSA can be available to survivors, but withdrawing early may carry tax costs, and your partner may need these funds for their own retirement.
  • Home equity is only useful if your family is willing to sell or borrow against the home. If the goal is for them to stay, do not count it.
  • Existing individual policies count in full, as long as you plan to keep them in force.

How your needs change by life stage

Coverage needs are not fixed. They typically rise as you take on responsibilities and fall as debts shrink and savings grow.

Because needs shift, it is worth reviewing your coverage after major events: marriage or divorce, a new child, buying a home, a significant raise, or starting a business. Some people layer two or more policies with different lengths so their coverage steps down as obligations decline.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Relying only on work coverage. Employer plans are often one to two times salary and may not move with you.
  • Forgetting the non-earning partner. Replacing childcare, cooking, transportation, and household management can be expensive.
  • Ignoring inflation. A benefit that looks generous today will buy less in 15 or 20 years.
  • Choosing an amount based on the premium alone. Decide what you need first, then look at how to make it affordable.
  • Never revisiting the number. An estimate made at 30 may be badly out of date at 40.
  • Not naming or updating beneficiaries. The right amount does little good if it goes to the wrong person or gets tied up in an estate.

Frequently asked questions

Is 10 times my income enough life insurance?

It can be a reasonable starting point, but it is only a rough rule. Families with a large mortgage, young children, or education goals often need more, while people with few debts and substantial savings may need less. A needs-based estimate is more reliable.

Should a stay-at-home parent have life insurance?

Often, yes. Although they may not earn a paycheque, the cost of replacing childcare and household work can be significant. Estimate what it would cost to hire help for those tasks over the years your children would need it.

Does my group life insurance at work count?

It counts toward your current coverage, but it is usually tied to your job and may end if you leave or retire. Many people treat it as a bonus rather than the foundation of their plan.

Do I need life insurance if I am single with no dependents?

Many single people need little or none. A small amount may still make sense to cover final expenses, co-signed debts, or to support a family member who relies on you financially.

How often should I review my coverage?

A quick review every few years is sensible, plus after major life events such as marriage, divorce, a new child, a home purchase, or a large change in income.
