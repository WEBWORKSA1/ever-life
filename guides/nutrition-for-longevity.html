Nutrition for Longevity: What the Evidence Says About Eating

A practical, evidence-based guide to eating for a longer, healthier life: Mediterranean patterns, protein with age, fiber, processed foods, alcohol and fasting.

By the Ever.Life Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-10-179 min readHow we research

There is no single food, supplement or diet brand that guarantees a longer life. What decades of research do show is that certain eating patterns, followed consistently over years, are linked to lower rates of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and some cancers. This guide explains what those patterns have in common, where the evidence is strong, where it is still thin, and how to turn it into ordinary meals.

Think in patterns, not superfoods

Nutrition headlines often focus on one ingredient at a time: a berry, an oil, a spice. In practice, the strongest research looks at overall dietary patterns, because people eat meals, not isolated nutrients.

Across large studies and the guidance of groups such as the World Health Organization and the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, the recurring features are:

  • Plenty of vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts and seeds.
  • Fats coming mainly from plant oils, nuts and fish rather than from processed meats and fried foods.
  • Moderate amounts of fish, poultry, eggs and dairy, with red and processed meat kept limited.
  • Relatively little added sugar, refined starch and sodium.
  • Minimal sugary drinks and, if alcohol is consumed at all, less of it.

The Mediterranean and plant-forward evidence

The Mediterranean-style diet is among the most studied eating patterns in the world. It emphasizes vegetables, legumes, whole grains, olive oil, nuts, fish and herbs, with modest dairy and little red meat. In the PREDIMED trial in Spain, adults at high cardiovascular risk who followed a Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts had roughly 30% fewer major cardiovascular events, such as heart attack and stroke, than a group advised to follow a lower-fat diet. A later reanalysis, prompted by randomization problems at some sites, reached a similar conclusion.

Other plant-forward patterns, including the DASH eating plan developed with support from the U.S. National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, have been shown in controlled feeding studies to lower blood pressure within weeks.

The common thread is not the absence of animal foods but the presence of a large share of minimally processed plant foods.

Protein matters more as you age

From midlife onward, people gradually lose muscle mass and strength, a process called sarcopenia when it becomes significant. Muscle supports balance, blood sugar control, independence and recovery from illness. Adequate protein, combined with regular strength training, helps slow that decline.

The standard recommended dietary allowance for adults is 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. Several expert groups that focus on aging, including the international PROT-AGE study group, have suggested that many healthy older adults may benefit from somewhat more, in the range of about 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg, spread across the day. People with chronic kidney disease or other medical conditions may need different targets, so this is worth discussing with a doctor or registered dietitian.

Practical ways to get enough

  • Include a protein source at each meal, not just at dinner. Many people eat very little protein at breakfast.
  • Mix plant proteins (beans, lentils, tofu, tempeh, edamame, nuts) with animal proteins (fish, eggs, yogurt, poultry).
  • Choose Greek yogurt, cottage cheese or soy milk for easy protein at snacks.

Fiber: the most under-eaten nutrient

Higher fiber intake is consistently associated with lower risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer. A large review commissioned by the World Health Organization and published in The Lancet in 2019 found that people eating roughly 25 to 29 grams of fiber per day had better outcomes than those eating less, with additional benefit possible at higher intakes. Most adults in North America eat well below that, often around 15 grams a day.

Fiber feeds gut bacteria, slows sugar absorption, helps manage cholesterol and keeps you full. Whole foods are the easiest source:

  • Swap refined grains for whole grains such as oats, barley, brown rice, bulgur and whole-wheat bread.
  • Add a serving of beans, lentils or chickpeas to soups, salads and bowls several times a week.
  • Snack on nuts, seeds, fruit and raw vegetables.
  • Increase fiber gradually and drink enough water, to reduce bloating.
Key takeaway: If you change only one thing, aim to fill about half of most plates with vegetables, fruit and legumes. That single habit tends to raise fiber, lower sodium and reduce ultra-processed food without counting anything.

Ultra-processed foods and alcohol

Ultra-processed foods

Ultra-processed foods are industrial formulations made largely from refined ingredients and additives, such as many packaged snacks, sweetened drinks, instant noodles, processed meats and confectionery. Large observational studies link high intake to higher risk of obesity, cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes and earlier death. In a tightly controlled 2019 study at the U.S. National Institutes of Health, volunteers ate about 500 more calories per day when given an ultra-processed diet compared with a minimally processed diet matched for available nutrients, and they gained weight.

Not every processed food is a problem. Canned beans, frozen vegetables, plain yogurt and whole-grain bread are processed but nutritious. A short, recognizable ingredient list is a useful sign.

Alcohol

Older research suggested light drinking might protect the heart, but newer analyses that better account for former drinkers and other biases have weakened that idea. The World Health Organization states that no level of alcohol consumption is known to be safe for health, and Canada's 2023 Guidance on Alcohol and Health describes two standard drinks or fewer per week as low risk. The general direction from health agencies is clear: if you do not drink, there is no health reason to start, and if you do, less is better.

Fasting and time-restricted eating: what we know

Intermittent fasting and time-restricted eating (eating within a set window, such as 8 or 10 hours) are popular longevity topics. Animal studies show impressive effects of calorie restriction on lifespan, but humans are far more complex, and long-term human data are limited.

What the evidence does show

  • For weight loss, most trials find intermittent fasting works about as well as ordinary calorie reduction, not dramatically better. A 2022 randomized trial in The New England Journal of Medicine found that adding an 8-hour eating window to calorie restriction did not produce significantly more weight loss over a year than calorie restriction alone.
  • The CALERIE trial in healthy adults found that modest calorie reduction over two years improved several cardiometabolic risk markers.
  • Some people find an eating window a simple way to cut late-night snacking.

What it does not show

  • There is no solid human evidence that fasting itself extends lifespan.
  • Preliminary observational findings presented in 2024 raised questions about very short eating windows and cardiovascular outcomes. These were not definitive, but the long-term picture is unsettled.

Fasting is not appropriate for everyone. People who take insulin or other glucose-lowering medicines, are pregnant or breastfeeding, have a history of disordered eating, or are frail should speak with a health professional before trying it.

A practical longevity plate framework

You do not need to track every gram to eat well. A simple visual plate, similar to the Healthy Eating Plate from Harvard and Canada's Food Guide, covers most of the evidence:

  1. Half the plate: vegetables and fruit, in a variety of colors. Potatoes and fries do not count toward this half.
  2. A quarter of the plate: protein foods, with frequent use of legumes, fish, poultry, eggs, tofu or yogurt.
  3. A quarter of the plate: whole grains or other high-fiber starches.
  4. Healthy fat: a drizzle of olive or canola oil, a handful of nuts or some avocado.
  5. Drink: water, unsweetened tea or coffee as the default.

For sodium, the U.S. Dietary Guidelines advise adults to stay under 2,300 mg per day, and the WHO recommends under 2,000 mg. Cooking at home is the most effective way to get there.

Frequently asked questions

Is there one best diet for longevity?

No single diet has been proven best for everyone. Mediterranean, DASH and other plant-forward patterns have the strongest evidence, and they share the same core features: whole plant foods, healthy fats, limited processed meat and sugar.

Do I need to stop eating meat to live longer?

Not necessarily. Research most consistently points to limiting processed meats such as bacon, sausages and deli meats, and keeping red meat modest. Fish, poultry, eggs and dairy can fit within a healthy pattern.

How much protein should an older adult eat?

The general adult allowance is 0.8 g per kilogram of body weight per day, and some aging experts suggest about 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg for healthy older adults. Kidney disease and other conditions can change this, so confirm your target with your doctor or a registered dietitian.

Will intermittent fasting help me live longer?

There is currently no strong human evidence that fasting extends lifespan. It can help some people reduce calories and improve certain risk markers, but trials show similar results to standard calorie reduction. Check with a health professional first if you take diabetes medicines or have other conditions.

Should I take supplements instead of changing my diet?

Supplements cannot replicate the combined effects of a healthy eating pattern, and most large trials of single vitamins for preventing chronic disease have been disappointing. Some people do need specific supplements, such as vitamin B12 or vitamin D in certain situations, which is best decided with a clinician based on your health and test results.
