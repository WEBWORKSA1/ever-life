Healthspan vs Lifespan: Why Healthy Years Matter Most of All

Learn the difference between healthspan and lifespan, why many people spend about a decade in poor health, and the five pillars that help close that gap.

By the Ever.Life Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-10-179 min readHow we research

Lifespan is how long you live. Healthspan is how long you live well, free from serious disease and able to do the things you value. For most people, the second number is the one that shapes everyday life, and it is the one we have the most influence over.

Healthspan and lifespan, defined

Lifespan is simply the total number of years a person lives. Healthspan is the portion of those years spent in generally good health, without disability or chronic illness that significantly limits daily activities.

Researchers sometimes use a related measure called health-adjusted life expectancy, or HALE. The World Health Organization uses HALE to estimate how many years a person can expect to live in full health, accounting for years lost to illness and injury. The difference between total life expectancy and HALE is a rough measure of the "gap" years lived with poor health.

The two numbers do not always move together. Medicine has become very good at keeping people alive with conditions that would once have been fatal. That is a real achievement, but it means many people now live longer with chronic disease rather than living longer in good health.

The healthspan gap and why it matters

According to WHO estimates, global life expectancy in 2019 was about 73 years, while healthy life expectancy was about 64 years. That leaves roughly nine to ten years, on average, lived in less than full health. A 2024 study published in JAMA Network Open, which compared 183 countries, found that the gap was widest in the United States at around 12 years.

Those years matter for several reasons:

  • Quality of life. Chronic pain, reduced mobility and memory problems affect independence, relationships and enjoyment.
  • Family impact. Spouses and adult children often become caregivers, which can affect their own health, work and finances.
  • Cost. Long-term care, medications and home support can drain savings that people intended to pass on or use in retirement.
  • Planning. A long period of declining health raises practical questions about housing, legal decisions and who will speak for you if you cannot.
Key takeaway: The aim of healthy ageing is not only to live longer but to compress the period of illness at the end of life into as short a window as possible. Researchers call this the "compression of morbidity."

The main drivers of chronic disease

A small number of conditions account for most of the years lost to poor health in later life. The WHO reports that noncommunicable diseases cause about 74 percent of deaths worldwide. Most of them share common risk factors, which is good news: one set of habits can lower the risk of several diseases at once.

Cardiovascular disease

Heart disease and stroke remain leading causes of death globally and in the United States, according to the CDC. They develop slowly over decades through high blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol, smoking, diabetes and inactivity. Many of these risk factors cause no symptoms until damage is advanced, which is why regular checks matter.

Cancer

Cancer risk rises with age, but a substantial share of cases is linked to modifiable factors such as tobacco, alcohol, excess body weight, physical inactivity and certain infections. Screening can find some cancers early, when treatment tends to be more effective.

Metabolic disease

Type 2 diabetes, fatty liver disease and insulin resistance raise the risk of heart disease, kidney disease, nerve damage and vision loss. The CDC estimates that more than one in three U.S. adults has prediabetes, and most do not know it.

Neurodegenerative disease

Alzheimer's disease and other dementias are among the conditions people fear most. Age and genes play a large role, but research summarized by the National Institute on Aging suggests that managing blood pressure, staying physically active, protecting hearing, and staying socially and mentally engaged may help support brain health.

A practical framework: the five pillars of healthspan

There is no single intervention that extends healthy life. Instead, the strongest evidence points to a handful of everyday foundations that reinforce one another.

1. Exercise

Physical activity is arguably the most powerful tool available. It improves heart and lung fitness, blood sugar control, mood, sleep, balance and bone strength. The CDC recommends at least 150 minutes a week of moderate aerobic activity, such as brisk walking, plus muscle-strengthening activities on two or more days. Older adults are also encouraged to include balance training to reduce the risk of falls.

2. Sleep

Adults generally need seven or more hours of sleep per night, according to the CDC. Short or poor-quality sleep is linked with higher risks of obesity, diabetes, heart disease and depression. Consistent sleep and wake times, a dark and cool bedroom, and limiting late caffeine and screens are simple starting points. Loud snoring or gasping during sleep is worth discussing with a doctor, as it can signal sleep apnea.

3. Nutrition

Dietary patterns that emphasize vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts and healthy fats, such as the Mediterranean or DASH patterns, are consistently associated with lower cardiovascular risk. Limiting ultra-processed foods, sugary drinks and processed meats helps. Adequate protein becomes more important with age to help preserve muscle.

4. Emotional and social health

Chronic stress, depression and isolation affect the body as well as the mind. Strong relationships, a sense of purpose and healthy ways to manage stress are associated with better health outcomes. If low mood or anxiety persists, speaking with a health professional is a sign of strength, not weakness.

5. Preventive care and screening

Many serious conditions can be detected or prevented early. Regular checkups allow tracking of blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar and weight over time. Recommended cancer screenings, vaccinations, and vision, hearing and dental care all contribute. Which tests you need depends on your age, sex, family history and risk factors, so it is best decided with your doctor. In the United States, the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force publishes evidence-based screening recommendations.

How to get started without overwhelm

Trying to overhaul everything at once rarely lasts. A step-by-step approach usually works better.

  1. Get a baseline. Book a checkup and know your blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar and waist measurement.
  2. Pick one pillar. Choose the area where you have the most room to improve, often movement or sleep.
  3. Set a small, specific goal. For example, a 20-minute walk after lunch on weekdays, or a fixed bedtime.
  4. Track for four weeks. A simple calendar tick is enough. Habits take time to become automatic.
  5. Add the next pillar. Once the first habit feels routine, layer in another, such as strength training twice a week.
  6. Review yearly. Use your annual checkup to see what has changed and adjust.

Healthspan also connects to planning beyond the body. Thinking ahead about advance directives, powers of attorney and how you want to be cared for means that, if illness does come, your wishes are clear to the people you love.

Frequently asked questions

What is the simplest way to explain healthspan?

Healthspan is the number of years you live in good health. Lifespan is the total number of years you live. The goal is to make those two numbers as close together as possible.

Is it ever too late to improve healthspan?

No. Studies show benefits from becoming more active, eating better and quitting smoking even later in life. Strength and balance training, for example, can improve mobility and reduce fall risk in people in their 70s, 80s and beyond.

Which pillar matters most?

All five work together, but regular physical activity is often described as the single most effective habit because it influences so many body systems at once. The best place to start is usually the pillar where you have the biggest gap.

Can supplements extend healthspan?

For most healthy people, there is limited evidence that supplements extend healthy life. Some people do need specific supplements for diagnosed deficiencies or conditions. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before taking anything new, as supplements can interact with medications.

How often should I have a checkup?

It depends on your age, health and risk factors. Many adults benefit from a regular preventive visit, and people with chronic conditions may need more frequent monitoring. Your healthcare provider can recommend a schedule that fits you.
